# Data for project 45

Synthetic. Generated by `_generate.py` with a fixed seed (`SEED = 45`), so a rerun reproduces every file byte for byte. Nothing here comes from a real bank, cloud provider, vendor or employer. Providers are invented codes (`CP-1` to `CP-4`), regions are `R1` to `R3` inside each provider, and service names are generic.

| File | What it holds |
|---|---|
| `services.csv` | The register as the bank holds it: 64 services, with the provider and region only where the owner disclosed them |
| `dependencies.csv` | The dependencies the register lists: 96 hard and 46 soft |
| `truth_services.csv` | What the generator decided for each service: true provider and region, true resilience, true standby, whether the standby takes over, whether the placement was disclosed |
| `truth_edges.csv` | Every dependency in the real world: 102 hard (6 of them not listed in `dependencies.csv`) and 46 soft |
| `truth_scenarios.csv` | For each of 12 outage scenarios, the critical services lost in the real world, computed by `lost_in` in `_generate.py` |
| `example_services.csv`, `example_deps.csv` | The hand-written 10-service example behind `python3 concentration_register.py --demo` |

## What the generator builds

Twelve platform services (identity platform, message bus, primary ledger database and so on) and 52 business services (13 functions in 4 forms each, such as "wire transfers engine"). Criticality: 25 critical, 25 important, 14 standard. Owners: 51 internal, 13 SaaS.

- **Placement.** Each service sits in one provider and region. The platform layer leans on `CP-1` (weights 0.55, 0.25, 0.12, 0.08) more than the business layer does (0.34, 0.30, 0.22, 0.14), and the first region of a provider is the busiest. This is a design condition: it is what makes a provider look small by direct count and large by dependency.
- **Resilience.** `single`, `warm_standby` or `active_active`, with weights that favour a second placement for critical services. The second placement is in another region of the same provider half the time and in another provider otherwise. A warm standby is placed on the same provider and region as its primary with probability 0.08 (1 service: S-49). A warm standby takes over with probability 0.70 and does not with probability 0.30; this draw produced 1 non-working standby among 15 (S-14), well under the 30% the parameter implies. I kept the draw. It means the failover miss in step 4 rests on one service.
- **Dependencies.** Each business service hard-depends on 1 to 3 platform services, weighted toward the first few, and soft-depends on 0 to 2 more. Some platform services hard-depend on the first two. 6 hard dependencies of critical business services are planted as unlisted: they exist in the real world and not in `dependencies.csv`.
- **Disclosure.** A SaaS owner leaves provider, region and resilience blank with probability 0.35, an internal owner with probability 0.03. This draw: 9 services, 8 SaaS and 1 internal, 3 of them critical.
- **Scenarios.** 4 provider outages (`P:CP-1` to `P:CP-4`) and 8 region outages (`R:CP-1/R1` and so on), 12 in all.

## Where the truth and the rules share an author

The same person wrote the world and the model. The generator's propagation (`lost_in`) and the tool's (`lost_set`) are separate pieces of code, but they implement the same idea: a service is lost when its placement is hit and no working second placement survives, or when a hard dependency is lost. The planted gaps are not in the rules: the tool never sees the unlisted dependencies, the true placement of undisclosed services or whether a standby works. Agreement on the rest is by construction, so the results are a ceiling for these rules, not a rate on a real environment.

## What changed after the first run

Nothing. The generator was run once with the parameters above and the tool was written after. I did not search for a seed.
