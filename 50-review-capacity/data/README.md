# Data for project 50

Synthetic. Generated by `_generate.py` with a fixed seed (`SEED = 20261024`), so a rerun reproduces every file byte for byte. Nothing here comes from a real queue, bank, customer or employer. The queue names are generic labels I made up.

| File | What it holds |
|---|---|
| `cases.csv` | 13,959 cases across 13 queues and 20 working days of 8 hours: `queue_id`, `day`, `arrival_min` (minutes after open), `handle_min`, `channel` (`live` or `batch`) |
| `queues.csv` | Per queue: `hours_open`, `target_wait_min`, `target_share` (share of cases that must start review within the wait), `max_utilization`, `shrinkage` |
| `queue_truth.csv` | The planted kind and the true number of reviewers per queue: `true_seats`, plus the late share and utilization of the replay at that number |
| `example_queues.csv` | Three rows typed by hand for `python3 review_capacity.py --demo` (assumptions, not a log) |

## What the truth is

Days 1 to 10 are history. Days 11 to 20 are the days that happen: 7049 cases. `true_seats` is the smallest number of reviewers at the desk for which a first-come-first-served replay of days 11 to 20 meets both limits of the queue: the share of cases waiting longer than `target_wait_min` is at most 1 minus `target_share`, and utilization (busy minutes over reviewer minutes) is at most `max_utilization`. Each day starts empty; cases still waiting at close are worked off after close.

The generator replays with its own code. `src/review_capacity.py` has another replay, and `tests/test_golden.py` checks that the two agree on all 13 queues. The generator knows nothing about the sizing rules, and the rules read only the case log and the targets.

## What is planted

Each queue has a kind. The kinds are the reason the data exists.

| Kind | Queues | What is planted |
|---|---|---|
| plain | 3 | Poisson arrivals, lognormal handling times with spread near 1. The case Erlang C was built for |
| variable_handling | 2 | One queue with a few very long cases among short ones, one with a long-tailed distribution |
| opening_batch | 2 | A batch of cases lands at open every day, about 30 and about 36, plus a live stream |
| bursty_arrivals | 1 | Arrivals alternate between busy and quiet spells of about 45 minutes. A hard case: the gap statistic the tool uses does not see it well |
| low_variance | 1 | Handling times close to constant |
| hard_negative | 2 | Look risky on paper and are not: a batch with long-tailed handling but a four-hour target, and bursty arrivals on a light load with a loose target |
| drift | 1 | Live volume is 1.4 times higher on days 11 to 20 than on days 1 to 10. A hard case: a method fitted on the history cannot see it |
| heavy_tail | 1 | One case in twenty-five takes two hours, so ten days hold about thirty of them |

Arrival counts, the batch sizes and the handling times are drawn from the generator's random stream; the means above are inputs, and the tool measures what was drawn.

## Assumptions

- 🟡 Targets are chosen by me, not read from a rule: most queues ask for 90% of cases to start review within 15 to 240 minutes (one asks 85%), set per queue in `queues.csv`.
- 🟡 The utilization cap is 0.85 on twelve queues and 0.90 on one. It is a policy choice standing in for "a reviewer should not run flat out". Nothing here measures review quality.
- 🟡 Shrinkage is 0.25 on every queue: a quarter of paid reviewer time is lost to breaks, training and leave. It only converts reviewers at the desk into a roster and is not part of the replay.
- 🔴 That any real queue looks like these is hypothetical.
